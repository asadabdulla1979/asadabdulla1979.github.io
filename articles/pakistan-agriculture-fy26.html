When the Economic Survey landed this month, the number everyone repeated was 3.7%, the fastest GDP growth in four years. Agriculture barely featured in the celebration, which is fitting, because agriculture barely featured in the growth. The sector managed 2.89% against a 4.5% target, the widest miss of any part of the economy. The easy explanation is the 2025 floods. The honest explanation is harder, and it is the reason I want to spend this note on the farm rather than the headline.

Here is the uncomfortable truth the floods conveniently hide: even in a good year, Pakistani agriculture runs far below its own ceiling. The distance between what an average field produces and what the best fields already produce, using the same varieties, is the largest unclaimed prize in the economy. And for the first time in years, the reform package announced on 29 June points directly at it.

SECTION 01

An average held up by animals

Start with what the 2.89% is made of, because the composition matters more than the number. Livestock grew a healthy 3.75% and now accounts for roughly two-thirds of the entire agricultural sector (Exhibit 1). Crops, the part most people picture when they think of farming, grew far more weakly, and important crops crawled forward by just 0.65% with cotton and maize contracting. The sector is being carried by animals while its fields stall. And livestock has its own version of the gap: average milk yields run around 1,500 litres per lactation against a global average above 2,200, a story for another note.

SECTION 02

The yield gap is the prize hiding in plain sight

Now the central fact of this whole note. Take wheat, the crop on which four in five farms depend. The national average yield sits near 3.2 tonnes per hectare. The genetic potential of the semi-dwarf varieties already growing in Pakistani soil is around 8.0, and progressive growers on irrigated land routinely harvest 5 to 7. The frontier is not a mystery and the seed is not missing. The median farm captures little more than a third of what the best farms, using the same varieties, already achieve (Exhibit 2).

The frontier is already growing in Pakistani soil. The whole task of reform is to move it from the best farms to the median one.
SECTION 03

We have been planting thirstier crops in drier places

Behind the gap sits a slow distortion in what the country grows. Over the last fifteen years, as the Agriculture Census 2024 documents, the cropping mix drifted in the wrong direction (Exhibit 3). Cotton, our great export cash crop and the feedstock of a textile industry that earns most of the country's foreign exchange, has nearly halved its share of cropped area. Sugarcane, one of the thirstiest crops grown anywhere, held its ground, and maize expanded. We moved acreage toward water-hungry crops at exactly the moment water was becoming scarcer.

Cotton's share of cropped area fell from 14% to 7.9% in fifteen years. And the constraint underneath all of it is water, which we price as though it were free. Surface-water availability has been falling, the Rabi season this year ran well below normal, and the Indus system loses a large share of what it carries to seepage and evaporation before it reaches a root. High-efficiency irrigation can lift yields 20 to 40%, but its cost and know-how remain out of reach for all but the largest farms. Until water is treated as the scarce, priced input it actually is, the yield gap has a floor under it.

SECTION 04

The wheat market the state walked away from

If you want a live test of whether Pakistan can execute agricultural reform, you do not have to wait for the new policy. It is already running, and it is not encouraging. Under Wheat Policy 2026, the government did what the IMF had long urged: it scrapped the minimum support price and stopped public procurement, handing the market to private aggregators backed by state-guaranteed margins of 10 to 16%. The safety net that had anchored the wheat economy for decades was removed before a credible replacement was in place.

The result was whiplash. At the May harvest, with no procurement floor, prices collapsed and small farmers sold into weakness. Within months the open-market rate had climbed well above the notional Rs3,500 official price, reaching Rs3,700 to Rs4,650 per 40kg by April 2026, so farmers who could hold their grain withheld it and those who could not had already lost out. Procurement machinery seized up: nine of eleven shortlisted aggregator firms procured no wheat at all. Sindh bought 65,000 tonnes against a one-million-tonne target. And the retreat is now permanent by design: on 2 July the ECC approved the winding up of PASSCO, the state grain agency that anchored wheat procurement for five decades, complete with a Rs4.2bn severance package for its staff.

Exhibit 4
Deregulation without a floor produced whiplash
Open-market wheat price, Rs per 40kg, vs the notional official price
The floor came off and the farmer wore the volatility. Growers sold low at harvest, then watched prices climb past the official rate they never received. Wheat area fell 6% and a further planting decline is expected. Wheat is a roughly $9bn crop, so the mismanagement carries real weight. This is deregulation done to farmers, not with them. Source: USDA FAS; Dawn; Punjab & Sindh food departments · price path indicative, USD at ~281 PKR
The state removed the safety net before it built the trapeze. That is the whole reform in miniature: the right destination, the wrong sequence, and the smallholder left holding the risk.

This matters for the overhaul because it is the same government, the same ministry, and the same season. A National Wheat Policy is now under consultation, alongside the broader reform package. The question is not whether the diagnosis is right. It is whether a state that just mistimed the most basic intervention in its most important crop can execute a far more ambitious agenda. The wheat file is the reform's first report card, and the early grade is a caution.

SECTION 05

Who actually farms, and who the system serves

Any reform has to reckon with who the Pakistani farmer actually is. The Agriculture Census 2024 is blunt about it: 97% of farms are under 12.5 acres, while a sliver of large landlords, around 5% of owners, hold close to two-thirds of the farmland. The median farmer is a smallholder, and the system, especially its credit system, is built for everyone but them (Exhibit 5).

Source: 7th Agriculture Census 2024 (PBS); illustrative cumulative distribution · USD at ~281 PKR

The financing system mirrors that skew. Agricultural credit hit a record, crossing Rs2.16 trillion in the first nine months of FY26. Yet more than 90% of smallholders still borrow from the informal market, and for the smallest farms formal lending is barely a tenth of what they actually take on. Record disbursement and unchanged access are the same fact seen from two ends: the credit flows to established, collateralised borrowers, while the median two-acre farmer stays outside the formal system. That is precisely the barrier a credit guarantee is meant to break (Exhibit 6).

SECTION 06

The overhaul names the right problems

This is the context in which the Prime Minister's announcement landed on 29 June, and the timing makes it worth taking seriously. The instructions, read against the diagnosis above, are unusually well aimed.

SECTION 07

The prize, if we mean it

Set the scepticism beside the arithmetic, because the arithmetic is genuinely large. Closing even half the wheat yield gap, on existing acreage with existing seed, would add more output than any plausible expansion of farmland, and it would do so for the median farmer rather than the marginal large one. Pair that with high-efficiency irrigation at scale and a credit channel that actually reaches smallholders, and agriculture stops being the sector that drags the average down in a flood year.

It is worth putting a number on the prize, if only to see its scale. No one expects Pakistani farms to hit their genetic ceiling; the frontier is an aspiration, not a forecast. But the gap between where we are and where the science already reaches can be valued, and the figure is striking. Take wheat alone. Lift the national average from roughly 3.2 to 5.0 tonnes per hectare, the level progressive growers already achieve, and output rises by around 17 million tonnes on the same land, worth on the order of 1.3% of GDP in a single crop. Push wheat to its full 8.0-tonne potential and the addition approaches 3.5% of GDP. Now apply the same gap, which afflicts rice, cotton, maize and sugarcane just as it does wheat, across all the major crops, and the aspirational ceiling reaches somewhere near a tenth of the entire economy, sitting unclaimed in the soil.

Agriculture is the only sector large enough, and far enough below its potential, to move the national number on its own.

This is the difference, over a decade, between a country growing at 3.7% and one growing at 5%. The Prime Minister called agriculture a driver of sustainable growth, and the diagnosis says he is right. But the country's entire record says the distance from a correct diagnosis to a delivered outcome is where the work actually lives, and where, four agriculture policies and many apex committees later, it has usually been abandoned. The wheat market this year is the freshest reminder. The target, this time, is the right one. Now comes the only part that has ever mattered.
