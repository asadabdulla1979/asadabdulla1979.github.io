Doing the Math on My First EV

In April, with petrol at crisis prices, I built a spreadsheet to see whether Pakistan's electric cars actually made financial sense. Then the budget repriced every column, and I put a deposit down. A full accounting of what a kilometre now costs, who the FY27 Finance Act quietly picked as winner, and a calculator to test any electric option against the petrol car it would replace.

Section 01

The April spreadsheet

This spring, with the Hormuz crisis pushing petrol past Rs 400 a litre, I built a spreadsheet: fifteen electric and plug-in models ranked by price, range, charging time, cost per kilometre, and after-sales cover. The purpose was less to shop than to see the economics whole. I posted the table on LinkedIn, noted that the BYD Atto 3 came out best for my use, and asked what I was missing.

Three months later, the answer is that the board itself moved. Petrol has come off its Rs 458 peak but sits at Rs 310.71, revised weekly. The FY27 budget spared electric cars and, through simple inaction, hit hybrids with a 25% sales tax. Carmakers have cut petrol car prices by up to a million rupees to defend share. Half a dozen new electric and plug-in models have launched since April, with some twenty more planned for the half-year. And I have put a deposit down, though not on the car my own table picked; more on that at the end.

One correction first, because this publication corrects its errors, including mine. My April table showed a 0.2-year breakeven for the Atto 3, measured against a larger, thirstier car than anyone cross-shopping an Atto 3 drives, at crisis fuel prices. Run honestly, against a top-spec petrol Corolla at 1,500 km a month, today's petrol price, and grid electricity, the payback is 3.8 years. That is the number this article defends, and it improves dramatically the moment your roof enters the equation.

So this piece is the April post done properly: what changed, what electricity really costs at the plug, and a calculator (Exhibit 3) to run your own numbers rather than trusting mine.

Section 02

The board reshuffles: what the FY27 budget actually did

The Finance Act 2026, effective 1 July, is the most consequential repricing of Pakistan's car market in a decade (Exhibit 1). Three moves matter.

First, electric cars kept everything. The 1% sales tax on locally assembled EVs survived, with the 1% duty on EV CKD kits and the 25% concessional duty on imported EVs under $50,000, all extended to June 2027. This was not a foregone conclusion: June reporting suggested IMF pushback on the 1% rate, and pre-budget drafts floated a 25% sales tax on imported EVs. Neither materialised; only luxury imports above $75,000 were touched, with a new tiered federal excise of 30 to 40%.

Second, hybrids lost everything, possibly by accident. The 8.5% concessional GST on hybrids (12.75% above 1800cc) sat in the Eighth Schedule with a sunset of 30 June 2026, and the Finance Act simply did not renew it. From 1 July, hybrids, including plug-ins, fall into the standard 25% vehicle sales tax regime. Toyota repriced within days (the Corolla Cross jumped roughly Rs 1.3 million), Honda has followed, and a Haval H6 PHEV now carries Rs 2 million more tax than in June. The promised Auto Policy 2026-31, expected in August, proposes 9% for hybrids; until it lands, the law is the law.

Third, petrol cars got cheaper to import and dearer to feed. Customs duties on imported petrol cars were cut sharply, combined tariff incidence on a 1000 to 1500cc car falling from roughly 76% to 52%, which will drag local prices down through competition. But the pump moved the other way: the Climate Support Levy doubled to Rs 5 a litre, the petroleum levy sits around Rs 70, and whenever crude falls, the levy rises to absorb it. Petrol at Rs 310.71, revised weekly, is not an accident of the oil market. It is fiscal policy, and the single strongest argument for electrification in this country.

Section 03

What a kilometre actually costs

Every EV argument in Pakistan eventually collapses into one question: what do you pay for a kilowatt-hour? The answer is five different numbers, and the spread between them is enormous (Exhibit 2).

Start with an honesty check most analyses skip. A household consuming 400 to 700 units a month pays roughly Rs 45 to 50 per kWh all-in after surcharges and 18% GST. But an EV charges at the margin, on top of everything the house already consumes. A family shopping for a Rs 7 million car is running air conditioners, past 700 units in summer before the car ever plugs in, so every charging unit lands on the top slab: Rs 47.69 base, roughly Rs 57 per kWh all-in. That is the honest price of lazy charging, and the figure I use; the calculator offers Rs 47 for moderate households.

One rung down sits an instrument almost nobody has written about. Under S.R.O. 279(I)/2026, a household can install a second, dedicated EV meter and charge off-peak at Rs 23.57 per kWh before taxes, roughly Rs 28 all-in. Commercial DC fast chargers retail at Rs 40 to 45 per kWh in Lahore.

And then there is the roof. In February, NEPRA abolished classic net metering for new applicants: surplus units now earn the national average energy purchase price, roughly Rs 10 to 13, while imports bill at full retail. Commentators called it a blow to solar, and for exporters it is. But look at it from the garage: every noon unit pushed into a car battery is a unit you were about to sell for eleven rupees, now doing work that would have cost twenty-eight rupees per kilometre in petrol. Net billing, whatever its intent, has made EV charging the single highest-value use of surplus rooftop solar in Pakistan.

Section 04

The payback machine

Running cost is half the arithmetic; the premium you pay upfront is the other half, and the past year has been kind to buyers and brutal to generalisations. Petrol benchmarks got cheaper: the Fortuner by up to Rs 2.57 million in December, the Sportage L Alpha by a million in June, the Oshan X7 by Rs 850,000 this month, with July's duty cuts still to feed through. Electric options multiplied at both ends: the Aion UT and MG4 arrived below Rs 7 million, cheaper than a Civic, while hybrid and plug-in prices jump as the GST expiry passes through.

So rather than a ranking that will be stale by August, Exhibit 3 hands you the machine: twenty-four electric, plug-in, and hybrid options against eleven petrol benchmarks. Pick the cars, set your kilometres, petrol price, and charging source. A tax selector prices hybrids at the current 25%, the expired 8.5%, or the draft Auto Policy's 9%; toggles add maintenance, insurance, and a battery-replacement provision; and you can line up a second electric candidate side by side.

Three findings survive any reasonable setting of the sliders.

The budget's fingerprint is visible in the hybrid column. At June prices, a Corolla Cross hybrid recovered its premium over a petrol Corolla in 3.9 years, a defensible purchase. At the repriced Rs 9.85 million, the same comparison stretches to 10.0 years, longer than most people keep a car. Plug-ins suffer alike: a Jaecoo J7 against the newly cheapened Sportage needs thirteen years of grid-charged driving to break even. One unrenewed schedule converted the entire bridge segment from sensible to indefensible, at least until the Auto Policy restores a concession.

Cheap EVs are the quiet killers. The market map (Exhibit 4) shows the bottom-left corner, cheap to buy and cheap to run, is now exclusively electric. The Aion UT costs Rs 250,000 more than a Honda City and erases it in about a year; the MG4 sits in the same corner. Below Rs 9 million, the question is no longer whether the electric car justifies its premium, but why anyone with somewhere to plug in would pay petrol prices at all.

Kilometres are destiny. At 800 km a month, an Atto 3 against a Corolla is a seven-year proposition, hard to defend on money alone. At 2,500 km, it pays back in under two and a half years on the grid and twenty months on solar. The EV case in Pakistan is a usage question, not a car question, which is why a single "EVs are cheaper" headline serves nobody.

Section 05

User feedback from PakWheels

PakWheels has quietly become the country's EV testing institution, and its expert tests and owner reviews agree on the essentials: real-world range runs 15 to 30% below the brochure, the Deepal S05 REEV posted the standout result at roughly Rs 13 per km over a 1,000 km test, and the Jaecoo J7's celebrated 1,200 km claim did not survive the myth-test. The pattern across reviews is consistent: delight with the drivetrain and the fuel bill, friction with everything around it.

The delight: a Lahore owner put 6,400 km on a BYD Shark 6 in three months on three or four tanks, charging "essentially free" from home solar; an Atto 3 owner says petrol cars feel sluggish afterwards; used BYDs trade at a premium. The friction, collected in Exhibit 5: a Rs 54,000 month-long wait for a taillight that costs Rs 700 to fix on a Hilux, brakes that slam when a motorcycle cuts in, insurance loaded 20 to 30%, no registration relief, resale buyers pricing in battery replacements they will probably never need, and a family stranded on the M-2 in June when every charger at the Bhera stop was dead at once.

Section 06

The verdict, and an audit of my own

Three months ago I asked LinkedIn whether I was missing anything. Here is the answer I would give my April self.

The economics have resolved in one direction. For a household with off-street parking and city driving of 1,500 km a month or more, an electric car is now the financially conservative choice: a sub-four-year payback on the Atto 3 class on grid power, under three on solar, about a year on the cheapest EVs. The same Finance Act that spared EVs their 1% rate wrecked the hybrid bridge through inaction, and until August's Auto Policy says otherwise, the rational shortlist is petrol or electric. What has not resolved is everything around the car: charging beyond the city is a single-point-of-failure system, after-sales networks are months old, and insurance, registration, and resale still price EVs as exotic. Those are the costs of being early, and they are why adoption will be led by households with secure parking and, ideally, solar on the roof.

Which brings me to my own ledger, because between the April post and this article I put a deposit down, and not on the car my table crowned. I booked the BYD Atto 2, not the Atto 3. The audit holds up: the Rs 1.7 million I did not spend buys range my city driving would not use, the premium over the petrol car it replaces recovers in a little over four years on grid power and just under three on solar, and early owner data (about Rs 10,000 of electricity per 1,000 km against Rs 35,000 of petrol) matches the calculator almost exactly. The honest risk sits below, not above: the MG4 and Aion UT undercut the Atto 2, and PakWheels calls the UT the smarter financial play. I am paying roughly half a million rupees for BYD's 6-year vehicle and 8-year battery warranty, a dealer operation that delivered a hundred cars in a day, and a known resale market. In a two-year-old market, that is not sentiment, it is insurance. Delivery will tell me whether I priced it right, and this publication will get the running-cost data when it does.

If you own one of these cars, I want your numbers: real consumption, real bills, real service experiences, good and bad. The comment thread on the April post taught me more than most of the published commentary; I expect the same again.
