Here is a fact that should be famous in this country and isn't. In 1990, an acre of Pakistani cotton produced 249 kilograms of lint and an acre of Brazilian cotton produced 147. We were the better growers, nearly two to one. Thirty-five years later Brazil picks 773 kilograms an acre and we pick 241, less than we managed when I was in school. My last note was about the acres cotton lost. This one is about the other half of the collapse, because at Brazilian yields, today's shrunken acreage alone would out-grow our mills' entire demand. The yield is the whole ballgame, so here are the numbers.

SECTION 01

Where we stand on the ladder

Start with the ladder (Exhibit 1). Australia picks 872 kilograms of lint per acre, Brazil 773, and the United States 408, much of it on unirrigated land. We pick 257 on the USDA's measure, 241 on our own books, and only India sits lower at 176, with the excuse that most of its crop is rainfed. Ours is nearly all irrigated by one of the largest canal systems on earth, and it still delivers roughly a quarter of the frontier.

SECTION 02

We didn't fall behind. We stood still.

And we can't blame geography, because the race used to be ours (Exhibit 2). Press play. Through the 1990s we out-yield Brazil comfortably; around 2000 Brazil's line takes off, cerrado expansion, modern seed, relentless agronomy, and it never looks back, while ours oscillates in the same 180-to-280 band for thirty-five years. A crop standing still is a crop every new pest and every degree of heat finds undefended.

SECTION 03

What the gap is worth

Now price the standstill (Exhibit 3). Hold today's 4.97 million acres constant, raise only the yield, and value every extra bale at what imported cotton actually cost last year. At 241 kilograms an acre we grow about 7 million bales, half of what the mills need. At the US level of 408 the same fields grow about 12 million, and around 460 we stop importing altogether, on land we already plant, with water we already divert, before a single surrendered acre comes back.

SECTION 04

What actually moves a yield

What moves a national average is what reaches the median acre, and the list is short; 1. Modern seed: our commercial crop still runs on first-generation Bt while virus-resistant genetics sit in trials. 2. Research money: the cess that funds breeding collapsed by two-thirds after the mills stopped paying. 3. Agronomy: the state plans on 1.6 bales an acre while disciplined growers beat it on the same soil, which is why extension has the highest payoff per rupee in this story. 4. Water and price: efficient irrigation within smallholders' reach, and a price worth investing against. The OICCI puts the cost of standing still at two to three billion dollars a year, and Exhibit 3 gets you to a similar number just by dragging the slider.

The yield agenda · what the median acre needs
  • Modern seed approved: stacked Bt and virus-resistant varieties out of trials, into shops
  • Research funded: enforce or replace the cotton cess; collections are down two-thirds
  • Certified seed at scale: penetration today is a fraction of the sown area
  • Extension revived: the cheapest yield gains are agronomy, not genetics
  • Water priced and piped: high-efficiency irrigation beyond the largest farms
  • A price to plant against: yields follow investment; investment follows price certainty
Source: USDA Biotech Annual 2025 · PCCC via Business Recorder · OICCI "Seeds of Growth" Jul 2026 · FCA

SECTION 05

The choice, again

Yields are the one lever that needs no new acre, no new dam and no subsidy to move. Brazil started this race at 147 kilograms an acre, behind where we stand today, and walked away from us in fifteen years. The land is ours, the water is ours, and the frontier is published in a table anyone can read. What happens next is, as it has been for thirty-five years, a choice.
