Pakistan Agriculture · Cotton & The Import Paradox

The cotton we
chose not to grow

This spring, Punjab planted less cotton than it did in 1966. Cotton has lost its price, its market, its seed and its water to twenty years of policy, and the acres walked to a crop with a guaranteed buyer. The import bill at the ports is just the receipt.

Between April and June this year, the farmers of Punjab planted 2.61 million acres of cotton. That's less than the province planted in 1966. Nobody flooded them into that decision. No virus made it for them. It was made in spring sunshine, one farm at a time, on expected prices, expected costs and expected water. And when that many farmers make the same call, it isn't a mood. It's a verdict on the crop's economics. Everything else in this note, the shuttered ginneries, the mills buying American bales, the import bill, sits downstream of that verdict.

So here's the argument I want to make. The collapse of Pakistani cotton, from over 14 million bales in 2004–05 to about half that today, isn't bad luck, and one good monsoon won't fix it. Four policies built it; 1. Sugarcane got a guaranteed price and a captive buyer while cotton got neither. 2. The tax code spent a full year making imported lint cheaper than our own. 3. The seed system ran out of money because the industry stopped paying for it. 4. And water is priced as if it were free. The weather gets a chapter in this story. Policy wrote the book. The mills, the exports, the 60 cents of every export dollar that textiles earn: all of it hangs off what happens in the field, so the field is where we'll spend this note.

SECTION 01

Two decades of descent

Look at the shape of the fall first, because the shape is the argument (Exhibit 1). We peaked at 14.27 million bales in 2004–05. We held above 11 million for a decade. Then the line steps down, and it keeps stepping down. Yes, the floods are in there. 2010–11 dents the line, and 2022–23 craters it to 4.91 million. But look at what happens around the disasters. Production had already sagged to 9 or 10 million before the second flood ever came. And when 2023–24 bounced back to 10.22 million, proof that the land and the seed can still do it, we gave the whole rebound back within two seasons. No flood. Just 7.08 million, then 7.05 million. A weather story recovers when the weather does. This one doesn't.

SECTION 02

The weather alibi

Every year the explanation shows up on schedule; the heat, the virus, the rains, and every year it explains one bad season, never the trend. The numbers behind that 1966 comparison, for the record: Punjab's Crop Reporting Service puts final sowing for this season at 2.614 million acres, against the province's own target of 3.2 million. An 18 percent miss, half a million acres less than last year, and below the 2.96 million acres the province planted in 1966–67. Cotton Outlook had already called the early-June area the lowest since 1983–84, and the final count came in under the number they were looking at. Remember, all of this happened at sowing time, before a single monsoon cloud showed up. The weather hasn't even had its turn yet. The retreat came first.

The long view says the same thing. Cotton had 3.1 million hectares in 2004–05. It has 2.0 million now. A third of the crop's territory, gone in twenty years, and you can watch the whole reallocation happen, year by year, in Exhibit 2. The fields didn't flood for twenty years; they got handed to other crops, hectare by hectare, by farmers doing the sensible thing with the incentives in front of them. And to be fair to this season, the earliest signals aren't all bad. Ginners received a record 527,900 bales by mid July, up 77 percent on last year, mostly because Sindh sowed early. But early isn't more. The cotton research institute is already warning about heat and boll shedding in south Punjab, and fast arrivals off a shrunken area tell you about timing, not recovery. One more detail worth sitting with: Sindh, the smaller cotton province, out-produced Punjab last season, 2.95 million bales to 2.75. The historical heart of Pakistani cotton is where the surrender is concentrated. It's also, and this is not a coincidence, where the sugar mills went up.

SECTION 03

The deal sugarcane got

So what were those incentives? Put yourself in the shoes of a grower in the cotton belt. Sugarcane comes with a legal minimum price, Rs425 per 40kg in Sindh, about $1.53, and the mills are obliged by law to buy your crop. That guarantee has existed, in one form or another, since 1950. Now look at what cotton offers you, no support price, no procurement, and right now, not even a functioning marketplace, because the Karachi Cotton Exchange, where this crop's price was discovered for nearly a century, has been sealed in a property dispute since December. So one crop hands you a floor and a guaranteed buyer. The other hands you pests, heat, and a tax story I'll get to in a minute. Farmers can read a contract and they chose accordingly (Exhibit 3).

Now the part that should stop a policymaker cold. You could almost defend this substitution if the favoured crop went easy on our scarcest input. It's the opposite. On PIDE's numbers, an acre of sugarcane drinks about 12.3 million litres of water a season. Cotton takes 3.6 million. That's 3.4 times as much. Field measurements in Sindh say sugarcane needs more water than wheat and cotton combined. Think about where we live. This is one of the most water-stressed river systems on earth, and for twenty years policy has paid a premium to the thirstiest crop in the rotation, with the water itself priced at roughly nothing for everyone. Go pour the water back the other way in Exhibit 4 and see what it could grow instead.

SECTION 04

The tax that finished the job

If the sugarcane deal pulled the acres away, the tax pushed the buyers away. This part is recent, precise, and entirely self-inflicted, so let me walk through it slowly. Under the Export Facilitation Scheme, exporters could import inputs tax-free. Fine so far. Then the Finance Act 2024 removed zero-rating on local supplies to those same exporters. Put the two together and from July 2024 you had this: a mill buying Pakistani lint paid 18 percent sales tax and waited months for refunds, while the same mill importing American or Brazilian lint paid nothing. For a full year, our own tax code preferred foreign cotton to ours (Exhibit 5).

Credit where it's earned: in July 2025 the government closed the loophole and put 18 percent on imported cotton and yarn too. The field is level again. But level at 18 percent isn't neutral for a domestic chain that lives on working capital. The tax now sits on everything: phutti, lint, cottonseed, oil cake. And this June's budget kept it there, despite ministers promising relief. You can watch the consequences in real time. Ginners who opened early for those record arrivals are already shutting down again. Lint that sold around Rs22,000 a maund in Punjab at the start of June was under Rs18,000 by mid-July, a slide of about Rs4,000, call it $14 a maund, in six weeks. How does this look from the operator's side? A ginnery is working capital on a clock. Pay 18 percent today against a refund that might arrive next year, and you're carrying a financing cost no thin-margin processor can survive. So what happens? The trade doesn't stop. It goes dark. That's my reading of the 1.4 million bale gap between the official crop estimate and what ginners formally reported last season: the cotton exists, the paper trail doesn't. And notice which part of the chain shrinks first. The documented part. The part that pays the research cess and files the returns.

SECTION 05

A seed regime running on fumes

Behind the price and the tax sits the slowest failure of all: the seed. Our commercial cotton still runs, overwhelmingly, on first-generation single-gene Bt. The kind the world adopted twenty years ago. Leaf curl virus has been grinding away at our yields since the 1990s, and the stacked, virus-resistant varieties our competitors plant are still stuck in trials here. Why? Follow the money. Cotton research is funded by a cess on the mills, and roughly 80 percent of them simply stopped paying after 2016. Collections are down two-thirds. The arrears sit around Rs3.4 billion (around $12 million), a rounding error next to the import bill. The research committee sued, won 63 of its 65 cases, and still can't collect. And the government's answer, so far, has been to move to restructure the committee altogether (Exhibit 6).

SECTION 06

The paradox, quantified

Now put the pieces together and you get arithmetic no exporter can be proud of (Exhibit 7). Our mills consume the equivalent of about 10.2 million US-size bales a year. Our farms now supply roughly five. The sea supplies the rest. Roughly every second bale in the spinning frame is imported. And this isn't abstract. For two straight weeks at the start of June, Pakistan was the largest buyer of American cotton in the world; in one of those weeks we took 68,000 of the 112,000 bales the US sold, and by early June our mills had already booked over 200,000 bales of the new American crop before our own season had properly begun. The raw cotton bill came to $1.27 billion in FY25. And before anyone celebrates the FY26 import number falling 43 percent, look at why it fell. Textile exports went flat at $17.9 billion. June's shipments were down 17 percent on last year. Mills bought less of everything, while imports of second-hand clothing rose 16 percent. That's not the paradox healing. That's the workshop shrinking to fit its imported fibre supply.

SECTION 07

What revival actually requires

Now, the government isn't blind to any of this. There's a revival plan, and its targets are on the table for the season already underway.

The 2026–27 season so far
  • FCA target: 9.64m bales from 5.33m acres nationally
  • Punjab sowing, final: 2.614m acres vs the 3.2m target
  • Record early arrivals: 527,900 bales by 15 July, +77% YoY
  • Ginners' verdict: the output target publicly called unrealistic
  • Karachi Cotton Exchange: sealed since December; no official spot mechanism
  • GST relief on ginning: promised before the budget, not delivered
Source: Federal Committee on Agriculture · Punjab CRS · PCGA · Dawn · Business Recorder · Apr–Jul 2026

A 9.64 million bale target on 5.33 million acres was ambitious the day it was set. With Punjab sowing 18 percent short, the ginners who publicly called it unrealistic will probably be proven right. But here's the deeper point: even hitting the target wouldn't end the paradox, because the binding constraint isn't acres. It's yield. We harvest about 635 kg of lint per hectare on the USDA's measure. Brazil gets three times that. Australia gets nearly three and a half. And both of them were once much closer to our level than they are now. I walked through this same arithmetic for wheat in my last note, and in cotton it bites harder, because every extra bale grown at home is a bale we don't have to buy at import prices (Exhibit 8).

SECTION 08

The choice

Cotton answers to contracts. To prices, taxes, seed catalogues and water allocations. Every one of those is written by the state or by the industry. None of them is written by the sky. That's the uncomfortable core of this note, and honestly, it's also the hopeful part, because what policy broke, policy can repair. Give cotton the same quality of deal sugarcane has enjoyed for seventy years. Take the tax off the part of the chain that documents the crop. Fund the science with something steadier than lawsuits. Price water honestly for everyone. Do that, and the acres come back the same way they left: one rational farmer at a time.

Until then, the paradox renews itself every budget season, and we should call it what it is. Pakistan hasn't lost the ability to grow cotton. We've chosen, through a pile of specific and reversible decisions, not to. The workshop still hums, three shifts a day, sixty cents of every export dollar. For now, it hums on borrowed thread.
